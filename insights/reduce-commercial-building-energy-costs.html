How to Reduce Commercial Building Energy Costs Without Increasing Engineering Workload

Reducing commercial building energy costs is easier to sustain when improvements fit the way a building is operated and maintained. For property owners, managing agents and facilities management providers, an energy-saving measure also needs to work within the capacity of the engineering team.

The opportunity is to reduce avoidable heating, cooling and ventilation demand while limiting repetitive optimisation work. That starts with understanding existing performance, choosing an appropriate approach and agreeing how results will be measured.

1. Where commercial building energy costs arise

An energy bill reflects both consumption and the price paid for that energy. Reviewing tariffs may improve purchasing costs; reducing HVAC energy consumption addresses how the building operates. Maintenance, software and service costs should be considered separately when assessing the overall return.

Common sources of avoidable HVAC consumption include:

  • Operating schedules that no longer match occupied hours.
  • Heating and cooling systems working against one another.
  • Plant running longer or harder than building conditions require.
  • Temporary overrides remaining in place after their original purpose has passed.
  • Control settings that have not kept pace with changing occupancy, weather or building use.

Start by comparing operating patterns with actual requirements. A quiet Friday, a busy meeting floor and a specialist space may need different approaches. Commercial building energy efficiency depends on maintaining appropriate conditions while reducing unnecessary operation.

2. The hidden engineering workload behind energy savings

An analytics report may identify excessive overnight consumption. Acting on it can involve checking occupancy arrangements, investigating the cause, agreeing changes, implementing them and reviewing the outcome.

Engineers must then monitor comfort, confirm that essential services remain available and revisit performance as conditions change. Across a portfolio, each recommendation can create several tasks involving site teams, controls contractors and facilities managers.

Identifying wasted energy is only the beginning. Someone needs responsibility for turning the finding into a safe, sustained improvement.

Dynamic HVAC optimisation can reduce the recurring manual engineering effort needed to turn those opportunities into controlled action and sustain improvements as conditions change. Engineers still provide oversight and resolve issues requiring their expertise.

Before adopting another tool, ask what work it removes, what work it creates and who will perform the remaining tasks. A dashboard that produces more recommendations may be useful, but its value depends on the capacity to act on them.

3. Compare the available approaches—and the work each requires

There is no single approach for every building. A controls review may resolve basic problems, while changing conditions may justify more frequent optimisation. Different approaches can also complement one another.

Approach Benefits and limitations Engineering involvement
Conventional BMS controls review Checks schedules, control logic and settings against current requirements. Can identify foundational issues, but a review alone does not keep settings aligned with subsequent changes. Engineers assess operation, agree corrections and confirm that the revised strategy works.
Periodic manual optimisation Allows targeted tuning using site knowledge and performance data. Improvements need revisiting as occupancy, weather and equipment behaviour change. Engineers investigate, implement and check changes at agreed intervals.
Energy monitoring and analytics Improves visibility of consumption, trends and possible faults. Recommendations still need an implementation route unless the platform also provides suitable control automation. Teams review findings, prioritise action and verify outcomes. Product capabilities vary.
Dynamic HVAC optimisation Uses building data and changing conditions to optimise selected HVAC operation through suitable controls. It requires technical validation, configuration and ongoing oversight. Engineers establish operating boundaries and retain responsibility for maintenance, faults and specialist decisions, with less reliance on repeated manual tuning.

Compare the complete workflow. Ask how recommendations become actions, how those actions are constrained and what happens when a fault needs attention.

4. Using existing BMS infrastructure to support engineering teams

Existing BMS optimisation can build on controls already installed. EcoAI’s dynamic HVAC optimisation works alongside the Building Management System, using live building data, learned behaviour and weather information to optimise selected HVAC setpoints every 15 minutes.

The existing BMS remains the control system. Its local control loops and safety functions remain in place, while optimisation operates within agreed comfort and operational limits.

This can reduce repetitive tuning without removing engineering responsibilities. Maintenance, fault resolution, changes to building use and operational oversight still require skilled people. Initial integration and configuration also need appropriate engineering input.

Suitability depends on accessible data, controllable plant and an agreed technical route. Working through existing infrastructure can avoid unnecessary replacement, but any required metering, connectivity or controls work must still be assessed and costed.

5. Assessing the commercial case and checking the evidence

A credible business case considers expected energy savings alongside implementation costs, ongoing fees, engineering input and any enabling works. Confirm the tariff assumptions used to convert energy savings into financial value, and distinguish gross savings from benefits after service costs.

Assess the commercial case before committing to implementation. Agree the measurement boundary, baseline, reporting period and treatment of weather, occupancy and operating changes.

For example, distinguish HVAC electricity savings from whole-building electricity savings. A percentage measured for a particular system should not be applied to the entire building.

At the University of St Andrews’ Scottish Oceans Institute, verified results for April 2025–April 2026 showed a 21.1% reduction in HVAC electricity consumption and a 9.2% reduction in heat consumption, using weather-normalised reporting and site meter data.

This is a university example, not a commercial office installation. It illustrates results achieved through an existing BMS; equivalent savings are not guaranteed elsewhere. These figures do not establish quantified engineering-hours or labour-cost savings.

Explore additional HVAC savings evidence, assessing each project’s scope, reporting period and measurement approach. Keep measured outcomes separate from forecasts or annualised performance.

6. A practical checklist for choosing the first building

Choose a building with an identifiable opportunity and enough information to test it. Before progressing, check:

  • BMS infrastructure and connectivity: Identify the platform, available trend data, access permissions and secure integration route.
  • Controllable HVAC plant: Establish which systems and setpoints can be optimised, and any technical limitations.
  • Operating patterns: Record occupied hours, variable use, seasonal demand and specialist requirements.
  • Energy data: Check meter coverage, historical records, gaps and whether HVAC consumption can be distinguished from other loads.
  • Existing performance: Review known faults, overrides and current control strategies to identify where avoidable consumption may remain.
  • Comfort and operations: Agree temperature, ventilation, occupancy and other operational boundaries before changing control behaviour.
  • Engineering responsibilities: Define ownership of configuration, alarms, maintenance, fault resolution and performance reviews, including the input required during implementation.
  • Financial evaluation and verification: Include upfront and recurring costs, energy-price assumptions, expected benefits and a documented method for checking results.

A suitable first building creates a basis for deciding whether wider rollout is justified. Some sites may need further information or remedial work; others may offer too little opportunity to progress.
